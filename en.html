Introduction

What do the four soils have in common?

Over a large part of Hungary, clay prone to volume change, soil prone to collapse, organic deposits and made ground are found below ground level. They may only be built on with due care and expertise.

Change of state after construction

Expansive clay and collapsible loess

Some time after the structure is completed, an external effect (most often a change in water content) changes their state so much that the building moves unevenly and is damaged. At first sight they look like competent soils.

Weak soils from the start

Organic soils and made ground

Their strength is low and their compressibility high, so they are generally unsuitable for carrying loads. They are relatively easy to recognise, as long as the ground investigation is thorough enough.

Water is the common factorSwelling, shrinkage, collapse, the soaking of made ground and the drying of peat are all caused by water. This is why most design rules end up being about how to keep water away from the foundations, or how to keep it at a constant level.
1
Swelling and shrinking clay

Expansive soils

Most lightly loaded buildings founded at insufficient depth on such clay are damaged sooner or later. The damage typically appears after a long dry summer, especially if several dry years follow one another.

What happens in the soil? clay minerals

High-plasticity clay consists of platy clay minerals. Water enters between the layers of montmorillonite (smectite), the lattice “opens up” and the soil swells. On drying the water leaves, and the soil shrinks and cracks. Kaolinite barely swells, illite moderately and montmorillonite very strongly. A surface cracked into a mosaic in dry weather is a tell-tale sign.

The building moves all year round seasonal cycle

When the water content of the soil increases again (in wetter weather, mainly in winter and spring), the swelling clay lifts the lightly loaded building, and the cracks narrow and often close completely. By the end of summer and in autumn the building settles because of shrinkage, and the cracks open. Without intervention this repeats year after year.

A related effect · frost heaveFrost heave causes the same kind of seasonal lifting and settlement: there, water freezing in the soil and growing ice lenses lift the footing, and on thawing the soil settles back. The other effects acting on shallow foundations – vibration, earthquakes, cavities, scour and frost – are covered on the External effects on shallow foundations ↗ page (in Hungarian), with a separate chapter on frost.

Why are small houses damaged? 83 / 13 / 4 %

83% of the damage occurs in single-storey buildings, 13% in two-storey and only 4% in three-storey buildings. A larger building presses on the ground with a higher contact pressure, and this counteracts the swelling pressure, which is above 100 kPa for these clays.

Hence the rule: make the most of the bearing resistance of the ground. The higher the contact pressure, the less the swelling clay can lift the building.

Have you designed the house well?

A look at the wider worldIn France, the estimated cost of building damage caused by shrinking clay in the 2022 drought exceeded 3.5 billion euros. In the United States, in an average year, expansive soils cause more damage than earthquakes, floods, hurricanes and tornadoes combined. British experience shows that thirsty trees (oak, willow, poplar) can damage a building from as far as 25 m away.

If the house has already cracked: underpinning in sections one possible solution

For a damaged house the aim is the same as in design: the load must be taken down below the active zone, into clay that no longer moves. One possible solution is underpinning in sections. First, a site investigation has to establish which layers are present and how deep the ground moves. The wall is divided into 1 m sections, and the excavation goes below the active zone first under the 1 sections, then under the 2 sections; above the open pit the wall spans like an arch. After formwork, the pit is filled with mass concrete up to 5–7 cm below the footing; the next day the gap is rammed tight with dry-pack mortar, and the remaining gaps can be filled with cement slurry through grout pipes. It is economical down to 2–3 m; next to a tree this may not be enough, so thirsty trees should be planted as far from the building as possible, or it is best to cut them down.

The three conditions of collapse switch them on

Collapse needs collapsible soil, load and water together. If all three are present, the building cannot be protected from rapid, sudden settlement by increasing the safety against bearing failure, for example by widening the footing. Wet, saturated loess collapses under its “bare” load.

Which soil is at risk of collapse?

1

Loess

Wind-blown silty fine sand with a loose fabric and a macroporous structure. The grains are held by weak calcite and clay bridges, which water weakens.

2

Loose, dry fine sand

And the transitional soils. In a loose state their grain skeleton can collapse into a denser arrangement when wetted.

3

Fresh fill

Loose, uncompacted fill younger than 8–10 years can also collapse. This leads on to Chapter 3.

How do we protect against it? 6 + 5 + 1

Protection against damage

  • Keep surface water away: proper landscaping, no hollows collecting water.
  • Roof water led away from the building in a closed drain.
  • Careful design and construction of service pipes, using protective sleeves.
  • Watertight floor in wet rooms on the lowest level.
  • A cast-in-place reinforced concrete ring beam and slab at every floor, designed for wall settlement.
  • Reinforced strip, continuous or grillage foundation.

For loads over a large area (e.g. a hall): ground improvement

  • Surface compaction with dropped tamping plates (3–5 tonnes, dropped from 3–5 m).
  • Deep compaction (vibroflotation).
  • Soil stabilisation, for example with sodium silicate.
  • Heat treatment, soil burning (abandoned for environmental reasons).
  • Flooding + preloading: collapse is forced in advance.
During constructionAfter excavation, foundation trenches and pits must not get soaked! In rainy weather concreting must start straight away, or the soaked soil below foundation level must be removed.

The waste of the past under the house

Made ground

The growth of towns and industrial sites has created more and more filled ground. Brickworks pits, gravel and sand pits, castle moats, slag heaps, refuse tips and low-lying areas have been filled. In Budapest, a huge amount of rubble had to be disposed of after the Second World War.

How dangerous is it? classify

The material can be soil, construction and industrial rubble, material with more than 10% organic content (spoil heaps, industrial waste), combustion products (slag, ash), household refuse and waste, or a mixture of these. Household waste (municipal waste) and uncompacted fill less than 8–10 years old are very dangerous.

The density and compressibility of made ground can vary within wide limits. Loose fills can collapse considerably when soaked. Ground-bearing floors and the partition walls standing on them load the fill directly, which is why they are damaged most often.

An invisible danger: gases methane · cyanide

Toxic gases (for example cyanide) and explosive gases (methane) can also form in made ground. The landfill gas produced by decaying organic matter is roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide. Methane forms an explosive mixture with air between 5 and 15 per cent by volume, and it collects in basements, under floors and at service entries.

Methane concentration in the basement

Drag the slider
Case study · Loscoe, England, 1986The clay pit of a former brickworks had been partly filled with waste. Methane escaping from the tip collected in the voids under a house and was ignited by the pilot light of the gas boiler: the house was destroyed and its three occupants were seriously injured. A rapid fall in air pressure (29 millibars in 7 hours) helped the gas to escape. After the incident, the management of landfill gas in Britain was fundamentally tightened.

Design guidelines

Much like those for collapsible soils.

1

Limited contact pressure

Depending on the state of the fill, the contact pressure should not exceed 150–200 kPa.

2

Water away

Water must be kept away from the building: roof water, services, surface water.

3

Uniform thickness

The fill under the footings should be of roughly the same thickness.

4

Three-dimensional stiffness

The three-dimensional stiffness of the structure must be increased (ring beams, slab diaphragms).

5

No extensions

Extensions should be avoided, because they settle differently.

Peat, organic silt and clay

Organic soils

After made ground, organic soils cause the most damage. In Hungary, organic deposits of various thicknesses have formed in relatively many places. Peats are the most dangerous, but silts and clays with a higher organic content are also unfavourable.

The ground that disappears Holme Fen, 1851–2024

At Holme Fen in England, in 1851, after the fen had been drained, a post was driven through the peat into the clay below so that its top was level with the ground surface. Since then the drying peat has been shrinking and decomposing: today the ground is more than 4 metres lower.

Recognition which one is peat?

Organic soils are easy to recognise: they are dark in colour (black, dark brown, dark grey), have a low unit weight (they are light), their water content is high (w > 50–70%, but it can be several hundred %), and they often have a fibrous structure. Which sample is peat?

The oedometer modulus is small (300–2000 kPa), so the same additional stress often causes many times more settlement in peat than in a sand layer.

The permeability (k) is low, so consolidation is slow. Because of the decay of organic matter, secondary compression goes on for a long time. Organic soil is also sensitive to heat, shrinks on drying, and varies in thickness.

With a good cover layer: a narrow, shallow footing influence depth

The organic layer is often covered by a cover layer in good condition (often sand or silty sand). Then the possibility of a shallow foundation should be examined first. The bearing resistance of the cover layer should be used as fully as possible: the footing should be as narrow as possible, because this reduces the influence depth, and less stress reaches the organic layer. The total settlement must not exceed the allowable value.

Further rules

near the surface

Soil replacement or a deepened shallow foundation

If the peat is near the surface, (partial) soil replacement is very often used, or the building is founded with a deepened shallow foundation on soil of adequate bearing resistance below the organic layer.

thin layer

A stiff, insensitive structure

If the organic layer below foundation level is thin, a structure of adequate stiffness and strength that is not sensitive to differential settlement can be built.

dangerous

Vibration and groundwater fluctuation

Dynamic actions and fluctuating groundwater are dangerous for organic soils: peat shrinks considerably when it dries out.

structure

Compact plan, flexible services

At service connections, the larger settlement of the building must be allowed for. Partial basements and extensions should be avoided. Design a building with a compact plan and three-dimensional stiffening.
